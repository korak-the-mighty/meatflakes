# Prototype 001 — chicken eating-experience proof

**Purpose:** learn whether the format tastes good and works on food. This is a small, immediate sensory comparison; it does **not** establish a safe shelf-stable product, a commercial process or a storage life. Follow current meat-handling guidance and use cooked chicken handled for prompt tasting. Do not leave samples at ambient temperature, mail them, or serve them as validated ready-to-eat shelf-stable food. A qualified food technologist should design any later process and safety study.

## Make one controlled set

Use one chicken batch and one seasoning base so texture is the main variable. Cook and handle the meat safely, then divide it into four small labeled trials. Choose household equipment only for sensory exploration; no drying setting is a validated preservation instruction.

| Code | Intended contrast | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — crispy | Thin pulled pieces, drier/crisper finish | Crunch and breakage into dust |
| B — flaky | Shredded or fanned-out pieces, less aggressive finish | Visible meat identity and bite |
| C — softening | A structure intended to soften in a hot dish | Pleasant bite after contact with moisture |
| D — fat/flavor | A small skin/fat variation for roast-chicken character | Flavor gain versus greasiness and handling difficulty |

These are sensory hypotheses. If D cannot be handled safely or consistently in a home trial, skip it and record why. Do not treat a dry appearance as evidence of shelf stability.

## Record before tasting

For each code: meat source and lot/date; starting and finished mass; piece dimensions or photo with a scale; seasoning mass; cooking and finishing equipment, time and temperature readings; yield; appearance; and any deviation. Photograph dry flakes and flakes on each dish. Keep any personally identifying tester data separate from public batch notes.

## Taste the same way

1. Taste a small sample dry while it is suitable for immediate consumption. Score **flavor, crunch, meatiness and overall desire to eat again** from 1–5.
2. Put equal masses of A–D on **plain rice, baked potato, instant ramen, scrambled eggs and pasta**. Use the same base dish and serving conditions for each code.
3. For hot or moist applications, note the bite at **1, 3 and 5 minutes**. Score softening, recognizable meat appearance, flavor, ease of use and whether the dish feels more like a meal, each 1–5.
4. Ask one behavior question: **“Would you keep this within reach in your cupboard if a professionally validated version existed?”** Record why, not just yes/no.
5. Compare at least the best code against a relevant commercial topping on equal serving mass when practical.

## Decision after one session

- **Continue** if one code is clearly enjoyable, remains identifiable as meat in at least one everyday dish, and has a specific improvement path.
- **Revise** if flavor works but texture, visibility or softening fails; change one variable per next trial.
- **Stop this format** if none is desirable even before safety and cost constraints are added.

Write the observations and raw scores in a dated batch log before choosing the next experiment. The next technical step is an expert feasibility call, not a home shelf-life test. See [the validation plan](../07_TESTING/VALIDATION_PLAN.md).
